I am not a theoretical physicist. I am an applied mathematician who reads fundamental physics in the evening, with a pencil and too many questions. This one has been following me for a while: what exactly do we measure when we say we measure time?

## A clock counts

Take any clock. A pendulum, a quartz crystal, a caesium atom. They all do the same thing: they count repetitions. How many times the pendulum has swung. How many vibrations of the crystal. How many transitions of the atom.

But counting swings is always counting *relative to something else*. The pendulum swung sixty times while my heart beat seventy times. The Earth turned once on itself while the caesium vibrated an astronomical number of times. We never read time itself: we compare changes with one another.

## Newton’s time

For Newton, time is absolute. It flows uniformly, regardless of anything that happens. Clocks merely approximate it, more or less well.

It is an extraordinarily effective assumption. All of classical mechanics, and a good part of the numerical simulation I have practised, rests on it: you advance by a time step *Δt*, then another one, as if a cosmic clock were keeping the beat.

## Time as a relation

Other readings turn the picture around. Carlo Rovelli reminds us that in general relativity time is not the same everywhere: it depends on the gravitational field, and so on where you are. Julian Barbour goes further in *The End of Time*: he proposes to describe the world as a succession of configurations, with no underlying flow of time. What we call time would be a way of summarising how things change relative to each other.

I don’t know whether Barbour is right. But the idea struck me because it echoes something I find everywhere in modelling: what matters are the **relations** between quantities, not the quantities in isolation.

> Time would express less a flow than a relation between things.

## What it changes in the way I think

I don’t draw a grand theory from it. Rather a few habits of mind:

- **Always ask “relative to what?”.** A speed, a duration, an acceleration only make sense relative to a frame or to another process.
- **Beware of the frozen frame.** Looking at motion from a global frame freezes the scene. Thinking locally, in terms of relations, brings back a wealth of viewpoints.
- **Keep the humility of counting.** Our instruments count. Our models interpret. Not confusing the two avoids many false debates.

I keep reading and scribbling. If you have reading suggestions on the topic, I would love to hear them.
