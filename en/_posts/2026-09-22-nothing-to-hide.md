Whenever the conversation drifts towards online surveillance, someone ends up saying it with a shrug: “Anyway, I have nothing to hide.” I used to think so too. I now believe this sentence rests on a flawed line of reasoning.

## It’s not your messages that matter

We picture surveillance as someone reading our messages. In practice, what is most valuable is **metadata**: who you contact, when, from where, for how long, how often. Data about data.

Taken one by one, these pieces of information look harmless. Together, they draw a disturbingly precise portrait: your habits, your relationships, your likely health, your likely opinions. Without reading a single line of your conversations.

## Anonymity does not hold

We are often told the data is “anonymised”. The problem is that at scale, anonymity unravels on its own. A study published in 2013 in *Scientific Reports*, based on the mobility data of one and a half million phone users, showed that four spatio-temporal points were enough to uniquely identify 95% of individuals.

Four places, four moments. Your home, your office, your gym, the Saturday café. That is all it takes.

> The question is not what you are hiding. It is who decides what will be used against you, and when.

## The motive is always noble

There is a recurring pattern: every extension of surveillance comes with a cause that is impossible to argue against. Fighting terrorism, protecting children, fighting harassment. These causes are real. But the tools deployed in their name always affect the privacy of the many, and they stay in place once the emergency has passed.

The European debate on scanning private messages is a good example: weakening encryption for everyone in order to monitor a few means weakening everyone’s security.

## Why “nothing to hide” is a trap

- **What is harmless today may not be tomorrow.** Laws change, and so do governments. Collected data does not disappear when the political climate hardens.
- **Privacy is collective.** Your data also reveals the people close to you: their contacts, their movements, their habits.
- **Saying “I have nothing to hide” means you stop caring about those who have something to protect**: journalists, activists, vulnerable people. And one day, perhaps, yourself.

## Where to start

No need to become paranoid. A few simple habits already change a lot: end-to-end encrypted messaging, a browser that blocks trackers, a password manager, and the reflex of asking, before installing an app, what it knows about you.

Privacy is not about secrets. It is about freedom: the freedom to think, to change your mind and to be wrong without everything being recorded.
